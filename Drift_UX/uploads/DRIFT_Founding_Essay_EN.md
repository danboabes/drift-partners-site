# DRIFT Partners
## Activity is not alignment

*Subtitle, two variants:*
- *On the slow loss of direction in cross-border programmes.*
- *On the slow loss of direction in programmes.*

---

A programme director sat across from me last quarter and described, in careful detail, a year that had gone sideways. The programme — engineering in one country, delivery in another, a third site contributing on a tight cadence — had been amber for three steering reviews running. Decisions made on Monday were quietly reopened on Thursday. One site believed a question had been settled at the last call; another had not heard the same conversation in the same way. Status was technically accurate and operationally misleading. Two senior engineers had disengaged without leaving. The steering committee was beginning to ask the kind of questions a steering committee asks when it is not yet worried but wants to register, for the record, that it could become worried.

None of the explanations she had been given fit. A consultant had recommended replacing the lead architect; she had done it; nothing changed. HR had run an offsite; the warmth lasted eight days. The programme had a freshly printed RACI chart that no one was using. She was working as hard as she had ever worked. None of it had moved the underlying thing she could feel but could not see.

Programmes rarely fail because people stop working. They fail because they stop moving in the same direction.

The thing she could feel is what we call **drift** — a loss of direction at the system level, where a group of people who individually understand their work, work hard at it, and want to do it well, nonetheless produce a collective output that diverges from where the programme leadership intends it to go. Drift is not poor performance. It is not bad strategy. It is not a team-T-shirt culture problem. It is the gap between the programme as observed and the programme as reported, and it is the gap inside which most attempts to fix the symptoms turn out to make the underlying problem worse.

## Drift does not start in one place

It can begin with a technical issue no one fully understands — the integration that is signed off but operationally incomplete, and that quietly forces a workaround onto every downstream task. It can begin with a missing capability no one is willing to name, where a senior contributor is operating at the limit of what their experience supports and the people around them have learned to compensate. It can begin with unclear decision authority, where a question that should be settled at one level is in fact settled by whichever forum happens to discuss it last — or by neither. It can begin with a structural contradiction, where two contributing functions are measured in opposing directions and the programme has decided, implicitly, to wait and see which one wins. It can begin with simple pressure that has exceeded the programme's capacity, where the volume of work routed through a small set of people is more than they can absorb without making mistakes that propagate. It can begin with a vision that has been communicated clearly and heard differently — where leadership believes the direction is shared, and the people executing it have, sentence by sentence, taken it to mean something other than what was intended.

None of these, on its own, produces failure. What produces failure is what happens next.

## The programme loses its ability to see itself

Once the gap between observed and reported reality opens, every signal that crosses that gap arrives at the wrong destination, with the wrong shape, at the wrong time. Cross-functional decisions are made on incomplete information that no one realises is incomplete. Escalations are absorbed by middle managers because escalating costs more than absorbing — and so the escalation never reaches the layer that can act on it. Reporting becomes truthful in form but misleading in substance: every metric is correctly calculated, every dashboard correctly populated, every status correctly reported, and the picture that emerges from them, taken together, is wrong.

Programme leadership, at this point, is operating on a model of the programme that is six months old. They do not know it, because the people around them do not know it either. The model is updated at steering reviews with selected data, polished through middle layers, and presented by people who genuinely believe the picture they are describing. By the time symptoms reach the steering committee, the underlying drift is six to twelve months ahead of the diagnosis.

Activity replaces alignment. The programme continues to act, but it stops acting in the same direction.

## And in cross-border programmes, the loss of visibility has a specific shape

Most programmes that drift hard are programmes that span at least two cultures. Engineering in one country, delivery in another. Headquarters in one, operations in another. A vendor on a third continent. The mechanisms named above operate in any programme; in a cross-border programme, every signal about them passes through one more filter before reaching the person who could act on it.

We call that filter **cultural mishearing**. Each side of a cross-border programme listens with its own cultural ear. The same sentence, spoken once, lands as two different sentences in two heads. A participant says *"I will adjust accordingly"* — meaning, in their cultural register, *I have heard you and I will think about it*. The other side hears it, in their register, as *I have agreed and I will do it*. No one is lying. No one is miscommunicating in the ordinary sense. The signal arrived and was correctly received — by two different decoders.

Cultural mishearing has two faces, operating simultaneously. **Cultural deafness:** I do not hear what was actually said, because I am listening with my own ear. **Cultural over-hearing:** I hear what was not said, because my ear is trained to supply the interpretation it expects. The two work together, in the same sentence, in real time. Both sides leave the call thinking they understood. Both sides are wrong in equal measure. The mishearing is symmetrical.

In a domestic programme this matters less. In a cross-border programme, every escalation, every status, every signed-off decision passes through this layer. A technical blind spot reported across the layer is not the same blind spot that arrives on the other side. A capability gap named on one side is heard, on the other, as a complaint. A decision committed in one room is, in another room a continent away, still pending.

This is where most programme diagnoses miss the work. A consultant with no ear for the cultural layer will list the five mechanisms accurately and then be unable to explain why the programme leadership cannot agree on which mechanisms are actually in play. The mechanisms are real. The disagreement about them is cultural mishearing, doing its work invisibly.

## The wrong responses

Most programmes, when they reach this point, recognise that something is wrong. People feel it. They talk about it. They try to fix it.

What they try, almost always, addresses the symptom rather than the cause. The symptom is visible; the cause is in the system, undisturbed. The symptom gets cured — and a new one takes its place, generated by the same cause that produced the first. Programmes that drift hard are not programmes that have failed to act; they are programmes that have already cured several symptoms and are still drifting.

The most common is to find the difficult person. There usually is one, because there usually is one. They are removed, and nothing changes. The next is to add a forum — a new operating cadence, a new review, a new dashboard — on the theory that the programme is missing structure. The structure is added and ignored, because it does not act on the mechanism producing the drift; it acts on the symptoms. The third is to run an offsite and rebuild trust. Trust is generally not the issue, and rebuilt trust does not survive the first week back at the keyboard because the underlying contradictions are still in place. The fourth is to bring in a new programme manager — on the theory that capability is missing. Sometimes capability is missing, but the new programme manager spends their first six weeks trying to understand a system that resists being seen, and arrives at the same wall the leadership did.

All of these responses share a common shape: they treat drift as a problem of effort, of structure, of personality, or of capability. Drift is none of these. It is a problem of visibility. Until the programme can see itself — including the cultural layer through which it sees — no decision the leadership makes can be sure of acting on what is actually happening.

## What actually works

Restoring direction in a drifting programme is unfashionable work. It requires three things, in this order, and each one is harder than the next.

The first is to **make the programme visible**. Not as it is reported, but as it actually operates. This means observation, not framework — interviews conducted under conditions where people will tell you what they actually see; signal sampling across the channels where contradiction is most likely to surface; and, in cross-border programmes, a deliberate read of how the cultural layer is shaping which signals get sent and which get heard. The output of this stage is a description of the programme that is recognisable to the leadership but new. If, when the programme leadership reads it, they do not see their programme more clearly than they did a month earlier, the work has failed and there is no point continuing.

The second is to **isolate the mechanisms producing drift**. A mechanism is not a person, a team, or a process — it is a pattern in the system that, when removed, would cause the symptoms to recede. Mechanisms come in five families: technical blind spots, capability gaps, decision breakdowns, structural contradictions, and information distortion. In cross-border programmes, every one of them is refracted through cultural mishearing — which is why the same five mechanisms can be named by both sides of a programme and still leave them in disagreement about what is actually wrong. Most programmes under drift have three or four mechanisms operating simultaneously, amplifying one another. The work is to name them, to evidence them on both sides of the cultural layer, to rank them by severity and addressability, and to refuse the temptation to act on every one.

The third is to **make the small set of decisions that, if held, will remove the contradictions producing the drift**. Alignment is not communication. Alignment is decisions that hold. Five to nine decisions, made by people in a room with the authority to make them, structured so that each has a single named owner, an observable success signal, and an observable failure signal — and committed to in writing, in language that survives translation across the programme's cultural layer. Most decisions in most programmes are not actually decisions. They are positions, intentions, or preferences. A decision is a thing that, if reversed, can be reversed deliberately rather than absorbed quietly. Drift is fixed by decisions of the second kind.

## And then it returns

Without reinforcement, drift returns. The system that produced it is the system that received the new decisions, and the same forces that produced the original misalignment will, given time, erode the new alignment too. The discipline that prevents this is small and unglamorous: a quarterly inspection of whether each prior decision held, replaced, or drifted; a lightweight re-sampling of the signals; an early intervention on the new mechanisms that have appeared. Done consistently, this is the difference between a programme that restores direction once and a programme that operates with direction restored.

The goal is to keep the programme seeing clearly under pressure — including across the cultural layer through which it sees. Everything else follows from that.

## A note on the work

DRIFT Partners does this work for one kind of engagement: cross-border programmes — distributed engineering and delivery, transitions, post-merger integrations, multi-site operations — where leadership has lost real visibility into how the programme actually operates, and is willing to grant the access necessary to make it visible. We do not run change programmes. We do not deliver decks. We do not redraw RACI charts. We work with programme leadership in a senior, embedded posture for a defined period, against a defined deliverable, and we are explicit about the conditions under which we will end the engagement. We are a small team. We will stay small.

We are also deliberate about how the work ends. **Anchor** — our quarterly review tier — exists to keep direction once direction has been restored, not to keep us on the programme. When three consecutive reviews show no drifted decisions and no new mechanisms, we propose closing the engagement. The discipline is now inside the programme; it no longer needs us. We do not bill for ceremony. The success indicator is not how long we stay — it is how cleanly we leave.

The programme director I described at the start of this essay does not appear in our case studies, because we do not publish case studies that name clients. She came back two quarters after the work was done and said, in a sentence that did her credit and did the methodology none, that her programme had stopped surprising her. That is the outcome we sell. That is all we sell.
